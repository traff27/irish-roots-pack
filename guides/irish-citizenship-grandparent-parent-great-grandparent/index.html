Irish citizenship through a parent, grandparent or great-grandparent

Last checked 30 October 2026 against the official DFA page. Rules and fees can change: always check the DFA page before you post.

Which route applies to you depends on who was born in Ireland and when your parent became a citizen. Here's how the DFA's rules work for each generation.

In short

  • Parent born on the island of Ireland: you're probably already an Irish citizen. No registration needed.
  • Grandparent born on the island of Ireland: you can apply to the Foreign Births Register.
  • Great-grandparent only: only if your parent was registered (or otherwise became an Irish citizen) before you were born.

Through a parent born in Ireland

The DFA says that you're automatically an Irish citizen if one of your parents was an Irish citizen at the time of your birth and was born on the island of Ireland. You don't need to register. If that's you, please don't pay anyone to prepare a Foreign Births Register application.

Through a grandparent

If a grandparent was born on the island of Ireland, your parent was born outside Ireland and you were born outside Ireland, the DFA says you can apply to the register. It also says your parents don't need Irish passports for this. Northern Ireland counts, as the DFA's wording is "the island of Ireland".

You'll need certificates linking you, your parent and your grandparent. See the documents checklist.

Through a great-grandparent

There's no direct great-grandparent route. What people call the "great-grandparent route" works like this:

  1. Your great-grandparent was born on the island of Ireland.
  2. Your parent (their grandchild) registered on the Foreign Births Register.
  3. You were born after your parent's registration, so your parent was an Irish citizen when you were born.

If all three are true, you can apply through your parent. If your parent registered after you were born, the DFA says you're not eligible through them, and registering now won't change that. Citizens Information makes the same point: citizenship through the register runs from the date of registration, not from birth.

Passing it on to your children

  • Children born after you're registered can be registered through you.
  • Children born before you registered can't.
  • If you're expecting, the DFA says the child won't be entitled if you're not on the register when they're born. Expectant parents can contact the DFA's Customer Service Hub, and should mark this clearly on the application.

Situations to check carefully

  • Grandparent born before 1864: the DFA accepts a baptismal certificate, because Irish civil registration only began in 1864.
  • Adoption: an Irish-born person who was adopted needs the adoption certificate from the central authority plus the original long-form Irish birth certificate. For an adoptive (not biological) link, the DFA suggests contacting it directly.
  • Parent with no surviving paperwork, or names that don't match: you'll need marriage or change of name documents to link the records. Our certificate ordering guide covers where to find them.
